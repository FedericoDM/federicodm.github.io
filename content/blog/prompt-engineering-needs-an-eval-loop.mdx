Most prompt engineering I see — including plenty of my own, early on — works like
this: read the output, feel that it's wrong, rewrite the prompt, read the output
again, feel that it's better. Ship it.

The problem isn't that this never works. It's that you can't tell when it does.
You've got a sample size of however many outputs you happened to skim, drawn from
whatever inputs happened to be at the top of the file, evaluated against a
standard living entirely in your head. Change three things at once, decide the
result "reads better," and you've learned nothing you can build on.

I ran into this on a classification task that was sitting at 65% accuracy. It
ended up at 87%. Almost none of that came from writing a cleverer prompt. It came
from building the thing that could tell me whether a prompt was actually better.

## The annotated set comes first

Before touching the prompt, I built a labeled dataset — real inputs from the
actual distribution, hand-annotated with the correct answer.

This is the step everyone wants to skip, because it's the boring one. It's also
the one that converts opinion into measurement. Without it, "this prompt is
better" is a vibe. With it, it's a number you can defend, and more importantly,
a number that can go *down* — which is the only way to catch a change that helped
one category while quietly breaking two others.

A few things that mattered more than I expected:

- **Sample from the real distribution, not the interesting cases.** It's tempting
  to fill the set with hard examples, because those are the ones you're thinking
  about. Do that and your measured accuracy stops predicting production accuracy.
- **Annotate before you look at model output.** Once you've seen what the model
  said, your sense of the right answer bends toward it.
- **Write down the edge-case rulings.** Half the disagreement in labeling is two
  reasonable people applying different unstated rules. Making them stated is most
  of the work.

## Then change one thing at a time

With a scored set, the loop becomes ordinary engineering: change one thing,
measure, keep or revert.

That constraint — *one thing* — is what makes the loop compound. Batch five
changes together and a 4-point gain tells you nothing about which of the five
earned it, so you can't press on the one that worked. Isolating changes feels
slower per iteration and is dramatically faster per point of accuracy.

The errors also stop being anecdotes. When you can group failures by category
instead of recalling them one at a time, patterns surface fast — and most of them
turn out not to be prompt problems at all. Ambiguous class definitions, label
noise, inputs that no prompt could resolve because the information isn't in them.
Those are fixed by changing the task, not the wording.

## What this actually buys you

The accuracy number is the headline, but it isn't the real return. The real
return is that improvement becomes repeatable. When the model changes, when the
input distribution drifts, when someone adds a category six months later — you
re-run the set and you know where you stand, instead of starting the guessing
over.

The eval loop is the durable artifact. The prompt is just its current output.
